A proposal for a year of design research

Resilient homes for Aotearoa New Zealand

I'm proposing a year of design research into how New Zealand homes can become more resilient to a changing climate.

Adaptive reuse, modular strategies, modern homesteading, traditional building and new technology, combined into urban and rural designs that add up to a more resilient New Zealand.

You would be my client and mentor, giving feedback one afternoon a month, as a developer with experience in sustainable building in New Zealand.

The goal is a practical handbook on resilient design for the architects, designers, city planners and builders who design and build New Zealand's homes.

How can residential design make New Zealand more resilient?

Resilient means four things a house has to do. Keep families safe through a climate event. Keep working through it: water, power, warmth and food. Be repaired afterwards, and adapt to new challenges. And become more self-sufficient over time, with its own food and energy. A home families can survive in, and thrive in, through an increasingly uncertain future.

Survive

Flood, coast, wind, fire and ground, read site by site.

Keep working

Energy, water, waste and food that carry a household through an outage.

Recover and adapt

Houses that can be repaired, adapted or moved, and the rules that help.

Self-sufficient

More of its own food and energy, year by year.

In short

  • Scope: single homes, in three settings: rural new builds, urban new builds and urban renovations.
  • Shape: about twelve months, about 30 hours a week. I'd run it like an architectural thesis, with reviews and a monthly pin-up.
  • When: April 2027 to March 2028.
  • What comes out of it: the NZ Resilient Design Handbook, a reference for architects, planners, designers and licensed building practitioners. Plus the design moves, and a brief for a second year that designs the house.

Why tussock

Tussock is a native plant of New Zealand. The tall tussocks, Chionochloa, are found almost nowhere else.

Tussock is resilient. It grows as a tight clump, with its growing parts tucked into the base, so it comes through fire and drought, and grows back.

It holds water when it's wet and lets it go slowly through the dry, which takes the edge off floods downstream.

Together, tussocks make a resilient community: a supportive network that holds water for everything below it.

The tussock is the way I want a house to be, and the way I want a community to be. One that survives, keeps going, recovers and adapts.

It's the idea of this project, in one plant. A year of resilience, a year of adaptation, a year of growth.
