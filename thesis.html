The thesis

The question, and where I stand

How can residential design make New Zealand more resilient?

I'll work across three settings, test them against four pressures, and learn from nine areas of research.

Three settings

Adaptive reuse in the city, new urban homes and new rural homes.

Four pressures

A changing climate, energy, food and water, and security.

Nine areas of research

From traditional Māori and Japanese building to modernism, brutalism, modular building, homesteading, technology, adaptive reuse and the Living Building Challenge. More on each below.

Every design move gets the same four questions. Does the house keep families safe through the event? Does it keep working through it? Can it be repaired after, and adapt to what comes next? Does it make the home more self-sufficient in food and energy? The moves that pass go in the Handbook, written clearly enough for any architect, planner, designer or builder in the country to use.

Why climate

Climate is where new thinking is needed. Floods, rising seas, stronger storms and fire are changing what a site asks of a house, and a lot of the guidance is scattered or missing. Earthquakes and ground matter just as much, but New Zealand already has strong seismic codes. I'll design to those codes, not try to reinvent them.

Resilient, not resistant

You can't keep every flood, fire or storm out of a house. So I'm not trying to design houses that resist everything. I want houses that take the hit and recover: they keep families safe, keep running, can be repaired and adapted after, and lean less on outside supply.

Scope

Single homes, in the three settings where most New Zealand homes are built or rebuilt:

Rural new builds

Bigger sites, more self-reliance, often further from services.

Urban new builds

Tighter sites, district plan rules, shared infrastructure.

Urban renovations

The houses we already have: what can be improved, and at what cost.

Multi-unit housing, subdivision and apartments come later. The Handbook closes with what scales up from the single home.

What resilience covers

  • Hazards, by region: flood and stormwater, coast and sea-level rise, wind, wildfire, and earthquake and ground.
  • Systems: energy and heat, water, wastewater, and food from the section.
  • Materials and supply: local against imported, and what keeps a build going when supply chains fail.
  • Refuge and self-reliance: a safe room, storage, and enough backup for a week on your own.
  • How New Zealand builds: the trades, prefabrication, consenting, and what goes wrong on site.

Areas of research

What I'll learn from, and what I'm looking for in each.

Traditional Māori building culture and design

Traditional Māori housing, building culture and materials have a lot to teach about building well in this land. I'll approach it as research first: what housing research already exists, and what traditional building can teach a wider resilient approach. I won't borrow Māori culture or style. Te reo terms will be checked, and a Māori advisor will review the work.

Traditional Japanese design

Japan has long built with earthquakes, storms and floods in mind. I'm looking at timber building, the link between inside and out, and houses made to be looked after and repaired over time.

Modernism

Simple forms, honest materials, and plans built around light, air and daily life. What it got right about living well, and where it fell short on climate.

Brutalism

Mass, concrete and durability: heavy buildings that hold heat and stand up to weather and fire. And the carbon cost that comes with them.

Modular building

Homes made in parts, in a factory or on site, that can be added to, repaired, moved or taken apart. A way to build faster, and to let a house change with a family.

Homesteading, old and new

How households have fed, watered and powered themselves, from early New Zealand homesteads to today's off-grid homes. What works on a rural section, and what scales down to a city one.

Old and new technology

Passive moves that have worked for centuries, like orientation, shading and thermal mass, next to new ones like solar and batteries, rain tanks and heat pumps. Simple first, technology where it earns its place.

Adaptive reuse

Working with the houses we already have. What can be improved, reused or adapted, and at what cost, before building new.

The Living Building Challenge

One of the most demanding green building standards, covering energy, water, materials and more. I'll study it for ideas and benchmarks. It's one area of research, not a standard this year is measured against.

What the Handbook is, and isn't

It's guidance. Every design move points to the Building Code clause or standard it still has to meet, and the current Code and standards always win. Where structure or ground needs an engineer, the Handbook says so. It doesn't replace one.
