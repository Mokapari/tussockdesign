The year

Three semesters of research and a final month

Twelve months, April 2027 to March 2028: three semesters of research, breaks between them, and a month at the end to finish the Handbook and present it.

Each semester takes one big question and three areas of research. This is the shape I'm proposing, and it's open to your input.

  1. April to June 2027 · autumn into winterSemester 1: this land and its pressures
  2. JulyThree weeks off

  4. NovemberThree weeks off
  5. December 2027 to February 2028 · summerSemester 3: lessons from design
  6. Christmas and New YearA break inside semester 3
  7. March 2028The final month: the Handbook finished and presented

Semester 1: this land and its pressures

The question: what does a New Zealand home have to stand up to, and where?

I'll map the hazards and climate zones across the country, and study what recent events show: the 2023 Auckland floods and Cyclone Gabrielle, and the Canterbury and Kaikōura earthquakes. Then the codes and standards each hazard has to meet.

  • Traditional Māori building culture and design: what building in this land has long taught, with a Māori advisor from the start.
  • Adaptive reuse: the houses we already have, how they've fared in these events, and what can be improved.
  • The Living Building Challenge: its ideas and benchmarks, read early as a reference for everything after.

Ends with the proposal review: a one-page thesis statement, the annotated sources and the hazard maps.

Semester 2: living off the land, old and new

The question: what does a household need to keep going, and what fails first?

The fieldwork semester, into the spring. I'll visit homesteads and off-grid homes, and talk with builders, suppliers and councils. I'll compare materials and systems by cost, carbon, supply and repair, across energy, water, wastewater and food.

  • Homesteading, old and new: how households have fed, watered and powered themselves, then and now.
  • Old and new technology: passive moves next to solar, batteries, rain tanks and heat pumps.
  • Modular building: how homes made in parts are built, moved, added to and repaired here.

Ends with the mid review: the fieldwork record, the comparisons and the first chapters in draft.

Semester 3: lessons from design

The question: what can design traditions teach a resilient New Zealand home?

Precedents from each area redrawn to one scale and compared. Then everything from the year brought together: the design moves drawn as diagrams, set against the three settings and the four questions, and the hazard-and-response matrix finished.

  • Traditional Japanese design: building with earthquakes, storms and floods in mind, and houses made to be repaired.
  • Modernism: light, air and simple plans, and where it fell short on climate.
  • Brutalism: mass and durability, and the carbon cost that comes with them.

Ends with the pre-final review: the Handbook in draft, the precedent library, the design moves and the matrix.

The final month

I'll finish the Handbook and present it to you. Then a brief for a second year, if one makes sense, and a handover.

Running through all three

The monthly pin-up with you, and the Māori advisor's review of anything that draws on Māori building and te reo.

Room to adjust. The full scope is more than one year can hold, so food and refuge stay shorter chapters, drawn from published work. The order of the areas can change to suit you, the seasons, or what the fieldwork turns up.
