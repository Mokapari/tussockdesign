Research design

How I'll research the year

Every topic goes through the same six steps. That way the work builds evenly, and every chapter of the Handbook stands on the same kind of evidence.

The method, every round

  1. Read. The key texts and the New Zealand evidence for the topic: BRANZ, MBIE, councils, the Natural Hazards Commission and the science agencies.
  2. Map. Where in the country the hazard or constraint bites, by region and by climate zone.
  3. Visit. Fieldwork: stays on farms and homesteads, site visits, and conversations with builders, councils and Māori partners.
  4. Draw. Precedent houses redrawn to one scale and one drawing style, so they can be compared side by side.
  5. Test. Each design move drawn as a diagram and checked against the three settings: a rural new build, an urban new build and an urban renovation.
  6. Write. A Handbook chapter in draft, shown at the monthly pin-up.

Design research, not yet the design

This year is design research. I'll find, draw and compare the moves that make a house resilient: a raised floor for flood, an ember-resistant envelope for fire, a water system for a rural section. I won't design a finished house.

Out of that comes a matrix: hazard and climate zone down one side, design responses across the other. It shows at a glance which moves suit which site.

Designing the house comes after, in a second year if it makes sense. That would be a modular base house, a simple core with modules that respond to the site, tested against every hazard in all three settings.

The monthly pin-up

Once a month I'll pin up the work, like a crit at architecture school. What I'm after is honest, practical feedback, like:

  • This is a strong idea, keep going with it.
  • You should talk to this person.
  • You should see this project.
  • This won't work. It's not realistic, or it costs too much.

Each pin-up has the same shape: one question, the evidence, three findings or design moves drawn, what changed in the Handbook, and what comes next. It keeps the work in front of you in an afternoon a month, and gives you a regular point to steer it.

Reviews and a panel

Like a thesis, each semester ends in a review with a set body of work, so the year can't drift. A small panel stands in for studio tutors: an architect, a structural engineer, a Māori advisor and you. The year closes with a final review, like a thesis defence.

Sources

Every fact and figure has a source, New Zealand sources first. I keep what a source says apart from my own reading of it, so you can always tell which is which.
